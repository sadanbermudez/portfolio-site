One photon or two

The short version · part of Research background

This page in under a minute

In plain words
A molecule can be excited by one particle of light, or by two arriving at the same moment. With two, the effect grows with the square of the light's brightness, so it happens only at the focus of a lens. Move the slider and watch it, with the optics computed live in your browser.
Why it matters
That confinement is what lets two-photon microscopes see in 3D, and what could let light treat a tumour without harming the tissue above it. It is the physics of my PhD, and of the experiment the AI agent runs on the lab-agent page.
What I built
Three interactive simulations: the glow of one- and two-photon light at any lens aperture, how deep red and near-infrared light reach into tissue, and an illustrated model of photodynamic therapy.
What I measured
This page computes rather than measures. At an aperture of 0.5 and 800 nm the beam waist is 0.51 µm and the Rayleigh range 1.35 µm, which you can check by hand. Two-photon excitation fades with depth twice as fast as the 800 nm light itself (about 3.3 mm against 6.6 mm), so its advantage is confinement, not depth. The therapy panel is an illustration, not a dose calculation.
Glossary: the technical words on this page
Photon
A single particle of light. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy: one 400 nm photon carries as much as two 800 nm photons.
One- and two-photon excitation
With one photon, excitation grows in proportion to intensity (∝ I); with two, with its square (∝ I²).
Numerical aperture (NA)
How steeply a lens focuses light. The higher the NA, the smaller the focus.
Beam waist (w₀)
The radius of the beam at its narrowest point, the focus.
Rayleigh range (z_R)
How far from the focus, along the beam, it stays nearly as narrow.
Penetration depth (δ)
The depth at which light in tissue has fallen to about a third (1/e) of its strength.
Optical window
Roughly 600 to 1300 nm, where blood and tissue absorb and scatter least, so light reaches deepest.
Photodynamic therapy
Treatment with light, a light-sensitive drug and oxygen. The light switches the drug on only where it shines.
Femtosecond laser
A laser that fires pulses a millionth of a billionth of a second long, packing enough photons together for two to be absorbed at once.

01 · The focus

Gaussian beam in water

Same lens, two very different glows

The same molecule excited by one 400 nm photon or by two 800 nm photons, through the same lens, seen from the side.

0.50

Why. The same power crosses every plane of the beam, so linear one-photon excitation is equal in each. Two-photon excitation per plane follows intensity squared over the spot, which falls as 1/w², so it is confined to about two Rayleigh ranges: axial sectioning without a pinhole.

The waist trade-off. At the same NA the 800 nm spot is twice as wide, but squaring the intensity narrows the excitation by √2. The gain is not lateral sharpness; it is that nothing outside the focus is excited.

w₀ = λ / (π·NA) is the paraxial Gaussian estimate; z_R = π·w₀²·n / λ with n = 1.33. At high NA the paraxial model is approximate.

02 · Penetration

the optical window

Why near-infrared reaches deeper

Below about 600 nm, blood absorption and scattering stop light within a short distance. Between roughly 600 and 1300 nm sits tissue's optical window. Fluence falls as exp(−z/δ).

So two photons gain no signal at depth. Their advantage is that excitation stays confined to the focus, wherever the focus is placed.

δ values: 630 nm ≈ 4.0 mm (brain-tumour tissue phantom, μ_eff 0.25 mm⁻¹); 750 to 800 nm ≈ 6.6 mm (breast tissue, μ_eff ≈ 1.5 cm⁻¹). Real tissue varies widely.
