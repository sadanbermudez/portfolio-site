Singlet oxygen, from a mouse to a molecule

Photodynamic therapy treats tumours and infections with light, a drug and oxygen. I built a way to measure the molecule that does the damage.

3 CM · THE ANIMAL

Light from outside

One photon acts along its whole path. Two near-infrared photons add up only at the focus, which can sit inside the tumour.

3 MM · THE TISSUE

Skin, fat, muscle, tumour

Blood and scattering stop short wavelengths near the surface. Near-infrared light, 750 to 950 nm (the phototherapeutic window), gets further.

100 µm · THE CELLS

The drug waits in the tumour

The drug, a photosensitiser (amber dots), builds up in tumour cells over hours to days and does nothing in the dark.

10 µm · ONE CELL

A one-micrometre reach

Singlet oxygen lives about 3.5 µs in water and acts within roughly 1 to 1.5 µm (dashed red), so it destroys whatever sits next to the drug.

1 NM · THE MOLECULES

Absorb, cross, transfer

The drug absorbs, flips to a long-lived triplet state and hands its energy to oxygen. Out comes singlet oxygen, which oxidises the nearest lipid.

10 µm · THE OUTCOME

Damage where the light was

With two photons only the focal cell is hit. That precision is why each drug's two-photon singlet-oxygen yield is worth measuring.

01 · Seeing it

1270 nm photon counting

Detecting a molecule that barely glows

Singlet oxygen gives off a faint infrared glow at 1270 nm. In water about one molecule in a million emits a photon, and infrared detectors catch about 3% of those. Williams et al. (2018) first detected it directly in a hollow fibre; my thesis built on that work.

02 · The PhD

hollow-core photonic crystal fibre

A lab inside a fibre the width of a hair

An anti-resonant hollow-core fibre: a ring of seven thin glass capillaries around a hollow core. The core holds the sample and guides both lasers through it.

How it guides. The thin capillary walls act as Fabry-Perot resonators. Away from their resonances they reflect light back into the core; at a resonance, λ = 2t·√(nglass² − ncore²)/m, light leaks out. Filling the core with water moves every resonance to about 0.55 times its wavelength in air, so the fibre carries both a 470 nm probe and an 800 nm pump in water.

Fibre drawing is schematic; the mode is the J₀² profile of a hollow waveguide. Membrane thickness is a free parameter here, not my fibre's measured value.

03 · The method

two-colour pump-probe

Counting singlet oxygen with a light switch

The 1270 nm glow is too weak in water inside a fibre, so I used a chemical reporter, Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green. It stays dark until singlet oxygen adds across its anthracene, then fluoresces about ten times brighter. Two lasers share the core: an 800 nm femtosecond pump excites the photosensitiser by two photons for 60 s, a 470 nm probe reads the reporter for 5 s, twenty cycles or more.

The reporter's growth rate gives the singlet oxygen made. Against laser power on log-log axes its slope must be 2 to prove two photons; in the final design every photosensitiser gave 1.85 to 2.09. Divided by concentration, the rate gives the figure of merit for two-photon therapy: cross-section times singlet-oxygen yield. The AI agent on my lab-agent page found the same log-log test on its own.

05 · What was new

  • The first use of an anti-resonant hollow-core fibre in photochemistry, and of the SOSG reporter ratiometrically inside a hollow-core fibre.
  • Photosensitiser concentrations down to 10⁻⁸ M, two orders of magnitude below the previous fibre work and low enough to avoid aggregation: a copper phthalocyanine reported to make no singlet oxygen turned out to make plenty as a monomer.
  • Two-photon cross-sections of clinical photosensitisers measured in water, many for the first time.

06 · The postdoc

Light4Lungs · IQS Barcelona

Breathing in the light

Light4Lungs, an EU Horizon 2020 FET Open project coordinated at IQS by Prof. Santi Nonell, whose singlet-oxygen probes I used throughout my PhD, set out to treat drug-resistant lung infections with "a novel inhalable photodynamic therapy": light-emitting particles breathed in as an aerosol. I measured singlet oxygen made inside bacteria with ultrasensitive photon detection, photodiodes and photomultiplier tubes, and coordinated the optics, biochemistry and modelling teams.
