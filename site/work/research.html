Singlet oxygen, from a mouse to a molecule

Photodynamic therapy kills a tumour with light, a drug and the oxygen already in the tissue. I spent five years measuring the molecule that does the killing, and the hardest part was seeing it at all.

Powers of ten

A photosensitiser is injected and collects in the tumour. Then light is aimed at it. Switch between one and two photons at every scale and watch where the action happens.

3 CM · THE ANIMAL

Light from outside

One-photon light lights the whole path through the skin. Two near-infrared photons only add up at the focus, which can sit inside the tumour. Near-infrared also travels further in tissue: 750 to 950 nm is the phototherapeutic window.

3 MM · THE TISSUE

Skin, fat, muscle, tumour

Blood and scattering eat short wavelengths near the surface. One-photon light excites photosensitiser everywhere it reaches, healthy tissue included; the two-photon focus excites it only where the beam is squeezed.

100 µm · THE CELLS

The drug waits in the tumour

Photosensitisers accumulate more in tumour cells, over hours to days. They are inert in the dark, which is the whole point: the drug does nothing until light arrives.

10 µm · ONE CELL

A one-micrometre reach

Singlet oxygen lives about 3.5 µs in water and a few hundred nanoseconds or less in cells, so it acts within roughly 1 to 1.5 µm of where it is made. Wherever the photosensitiser sits, mitochondria or membranes, is what gets destroyed.

1 NM · THE MOLECULES

Absorb, cross, transfer

The photosensitiser absorbs, crosses to its long-lived triplet state, and on collision hands its energy to ground-state oxygen, itself a triplet. Spin is conserved, so only the triplet can do it. The oxygen leaves as singlet oxygen and oxidises the first lipid or protein it meets.

10 µm · THE OUTCOME

Damage where the light was

With two-photon excitation only the focal cell is damaged; its neighbours never saw a high enough photon density. That precision, plus deeper reach, is why two-photon PDT matters, and why measuring a molecule's two-photon singlet-oxygen yield matters even more.

01 · Seeing it

1270 nm photon counting

Detecting a molecule that barely glows

Singlet oxygen gives one faint infrared glow at 1270 nm as it decays. In water only about one in a million molecules emits a photon, and infrared detectors catch about 3% of those. Below, single photons arrive at a photomultiplier and build the time-resolved signal.

The rise is the photosensitiser's triplet handing over its energy; the decay is singlet oxygen's own lifetime, set by the solvent. Heavy water lets it live about twenty times longer, which is why many experiments use it, and why measuring in plain water, as cells require, is so much harder. In my postdoc in Light4Lungs I detected singlet oxygen inside bacteria with high-sensitivity photon detection, photodiodes and photomultipliers.

02 · The PhD

hollow-core photonic crystal fibre

A lab inside a fibre the width of a hair

Two-photon excitation only happens in a femtolitre focus, so in a cuvette the signal drowns in noise. My solution: fill the hollow core of a photonic crystal fibre with the photosensitiser solution, and guide the laser through it. The excitation is sustained along the whole fibre instead of one tiny spot.

How it guides. The thin glass walls of the ring of capillaries act as Fabry-Perot resonators. Away from their resonances they reflect light back into the core; at a resonance, λ = 2t·√(nglass² − ncore²)/m, light leaks out. Filling the core with water moves every resonance to about 0.55 times its wavelength in air, which is how the fibre can carry both a 470 nm probe and an 800 nm pump in water.

Why it wins. In the thesis's comparison for a 20 µm core and 15 cm length, the light-matter interaction figure is 4.18 for a 1 cm cuvette, 16.4 for a glass capillary and 14,720 for a hollow-core fibre. The 11 cm fibre I used held about 2.8 nL per cm, six orders of magnitude less sample than a cuvette.

03 · The method

two-colour pump-probe

Counting singlet oxygen with a light switch

Direct 1270 nm detection is too weak in water inside a fibre, so I used a chemical reporter, Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green. It is dark until singlet oxygen adds across its anthracene, then it fluoresces about ten times brighter. Two lasers share the core: an 800 nm femtosecond pump excites the photosensitiser by two photons for 60 s, a 470 nm probe reads the reporter for 5 s, twenty cycles or more.

The reporter's growth rate gives the singlet oxygen made. Plotted against laser power on log-log axes, its slope must be 2 to prove a two-photon process. In the final design every photosensitiser gave a slope between 1.85 and 2.09. Divided by concentration, the rate gives the figure of merit for two-photon therapy: cross-section times singlet-oxygen yield.

The same log-log test is what the AI agent on my lab-agent page worked out for itself, unprompted.

05 · What was new

  • The first quantitative measurement of two-photon photosensitised singlet oxygen in aqueous solution, in any optical system.
  • The first use of an anti-resonant hollow-core fibre in photochemistry, and of the SOSG reporter ratiometrically inside a hollow-core fibre.
  • Photosensitiser concentrations down to 10⁻⁸ M, two orders of magnitude below the previous fibre work, low enough to avoid aggregation: a copper phthalocyanine reported to make no singlet oxygen turned out to make plenty as a monomer.
  • Two-photon cross-sections of clinical photosensitisers measured in water, many for the first time.

06 · The postdoc

Light4Lungs · IQS Barcelona

Breathing in the light

Light4Lungs, an EU Horizon 2020 FET Open project coordinated at IQS by Prof. Santi Nonell, whose singlet-oxygen probes I had used throughout my PhD, set out to treat drug-resistant lung infections with "a novel inhalable photodynamic therapy": persistent-luminescence particles delivered by aerosol, so the light source itself is breathed in. My work there was ultrasensitive photon detection, measuring singlet oxygen produced inside bacteria with photodiodes and photomultiplier tubes, and coordinating the optics, biochemistry and modelling teams.
